Tip It In
Pond Hopping · what “Add a trip” opens

Tip it in

Six routes, each with a paragraph, then two buttons. About 150 words before anybody can do anything — on the screen whose entire job is “give me something and I’ll work it out”.

The contradiction

The pitch is “whatever you already have”. The sheet makes you file it first.

Card one says “Whatever you already have — it works the trip out from there.” Then this opens and asks you to sort what you have into six categories and read a paragraph about each to know which one you are. That is a filing decision, made by the person, before the app has done anything for them.

What the research says, and what changed because of it

Show the transformation, not the feature list

The 2026 consensus on visual-first onboarding is narrower than “use more pictures”: dynamic visuals beat static tutorials, and the pattern that works is a before and after — fitness apps show the transformation rather than listing features, because one image carries what a paragraph cannot.

Pond Hopping's transformation is exact and easy to draw: a pile of photographs becomes a finished trip. That is literally what happens when somebody taps the hero, so the hero shows it instead of describing it — six thumbnails, an arrow, a trip card with a route on it. It is also, word for word, what the retired card two was trying to say.

Still too wordy? Yes — and now it is twenty

The hero drops its sentence entirely; the drawing says it. Three of the four rows keep a short hint only where the label alone would not land — “years of trips, all at once” earns its five words, “Paste a confirmation” does not need any.

The order was wrong, and so was the axis

It was sorted by what format your data is in, which takes thought. It should be sorted by when, which you know without thinking: on one now, one you have taken, one coming up, all of them at once.

“On a trip right now” is back

It was the sheet's first option and this page had dropped it. It is also the state the app is best at — live flights, the evening look-back — so it sits directly under the hero rather than in the quiet list.

Two buttons gone

Create an account is a second path to somewhere every route already leads. Have a look round first only existed to escape it. Remove the first and the second is an exit from a room with no walls.

The one that is still open

Where Google Timeline sits

It has moved below “forward a booking” in the ordering above, because “years of trips at once” is not a when — it is a different scale of thing entirely. It is niche in awareness and enormous in value, which is an awkward combination to place: bury it and nobody finds the best trick in the app; lead with it and most people read five words about a file format they have never exported.

The version drawn here keeps it in the quiet list and lets its five words do the work. Worth arguing about — it may deserve to sit beside the hero rather than under the fold.

The question underneath the others

No — this should not be the end of onboarding

On “should this close the first launch?”

Making it the last step puts a six-option filing decision in front of somebody who arrived forty seconds ago. Even trimmed to one target, it is a destination, not a step.

First launch is the cold open, the example trip, and your three cards. Tip it in sits there permanently and opens this whenever they are ready — which for a lot of people is the second session, after they have read somebody else’s trip to Rome and thought I want that for mine.

Which is the same shape as the recap and the same shape as the tour fix: the thing that earns the action goes first, and the ask comes second.

The entry point

“Add a trip” is a filing verb

It describes bookkeeping, sitting next to a card showing somebody’s actual holiday. It is also the only thing a new hopper can do, so it ought to be the most inviting thing on the screen. The strap underneath is already doing better work than the title.

A — Where have you been?

The most human of the five, and the only one that is a question, which is hard to walk past. It also happens to be exactly what the app is asking. Risk: a question on a tile can read as a prompt rather than a button.

B — Get your trips back

The strongest promise, and it speaks to the retro case, which is the wow. “Back” is the word doing the work — it says these already exist and you are missing them. Risk: means least to somebody with no history yet.

C — Tip it in

Already the best line in the product, and it makes the tile and the sheet it opens one thought instead of two. Risk: it is a phrase, not a description — charming to somebody who has read the card, opaque to somebody who has not.

D — Yours goes here

Uses the one thing this layout has that no copy can buy: it sits directly beside a finished example, under a rail already labelled YOURS. Says the whole thing in three words by pointing.

If it were mine

B, and C for the sheet it opens. “Get your trips back” is the only one that promises something a person did not know they could have, and “back” does more work than any other word on the screen. Then Tip it in is what the sheet is called, so the phrase still lands — one beat later, where it is an instruction rather than a riddle.

D is the one I would test against it. It is weaker on its own and much stronger in place, which is a hard thing to judge from a mockup and an easy thing to judge on a phone.

Decided this round

Where the proposal stands

  • The cards come down to one, and the other two are parked, not deleted — their copy kept somewhere findable for later.
  • Cold open: first launch only. Not every time.
  • The tour goes. Its one irreplaceable line — “someone else’s pond” — moves onto the example trip itself.
  • The sheet is rebuilt as above: one target, four one-liners, no buttons.
  • Partner email: gone. Never seen in the wild. It comes back when somebody uploads or plans a trip — which is a better moment anyway, because that is when there is a second person to name and a reason to bring them in.
